Case Study

AVALX Pronunciation Tool

Designing and evaluating an accessible pronunciation learning tool for Persian-speaking EFL learners.

01

Overview

AVALX is a multimedia pronunciation tool that helps Iranian EFL learners improve their pronunciation through visual, auditory, and contextual practice. It was built for learners who may not have access to native-speaking environments, tutoring, or high-bandwidth platforms.

The project tested whether a custom-designed tool could help learners improve more than a standard computerized dictionary, and explored how to design pronunciation technology with accessibility, localization, and learner autonomy in mind.

02

The problem

Some English sounds, like /w/, /ð/, and /θ/, don't exist in Farsi, so learners often replace them with familiar sounds. At the same time, most pronunciation tools assume stable internet, strong digital literacy, English-only navigation, and confidence with unfamiliar software. Those assumptions exclude learners in under-resourced or linguistically diverse settings.

How might we help beginner EFL learners practice difficult English sounds on their own, while reducing barriers of access, language, usability, and cognitive load?

03

My role

My role combined UX research, instructional design, and educational technology design. I:

  • Identified pronunciation challenges for Farsi-speaking learners
  • Designed the structure and learning flow of AVALX
  • Created the multimedia learning experience, interactive activities, practice sequences, and tests
  • Supported the evaluation through a controlled study with experimental and control groups
  • Analyzed and interpreted results through pedagogical and equity lenses
04

Research questions

Compared with a computerized dictionary, does multimedia instruction improve learners':

  • Knowledge of English phonetic symbols?
  • Ability to discriminate between English phonemes?
  • Intonation in Yes/No questions, Wh-questions, and statements?
  • Pronunciation of /w/, /ð/, and /θ/?
05

Design solution

Phonetic chart

40 symbols, with only 6–7 active at a time for gradual progress.

Audio playback

Every symbol can be replayed as often as needed.

Articulation videos

Short clips of a speaker showing how each sound is made.

Sagittal animations

Diagrams of how the vocal organs move for each sound.

Words in context

Ten example words per sound, with transcription and film clips.

Offline submission

Results can be emailed or saved locally without internet.

06

Key design decisions

Reducing cognitive load

Activating only a few symbols at a time kept beginners focused instead of overwhelmed.

Multiple learning modalities

Sound, text, symbols, diagrams, videos, and film clips help learners connect what they hear, see, and say.

Localizing the experience

Farsi-language guidance and visual help removed the barrier of English-only instructions.

Designing for low bandwidth

The tool installs and runs offline, so learners don't need reliable internet.

Encouraging autonomy

Learners can repeat, replay, and move at their own pace, with short quizzes before each new set.

07

Findings

Both groups improved, but learners using AVALX showed significantly stronger gains in:

  • Recognizing phonetic symbols
  • Discriminating between phonemes
  • Producing difficult consonants: /w/, /ð/, and /θ/
  • Intonation in questions and statements

In UX terms A localized, multimedia tool improved both learning performance and independent practice compared with a general-purpose reference tool.

08

Impact

Access

Usable without constant internet access.

Usability

Simpler navigation with fewer active items at once.

Language

Farsi support made the tool easy to understand.

Learning

Visual, audio, and contextual supports for hard sounds.

Inclusive design should ask not only whether a tool works, but who it works for, under what conditions, and with what support.

09

What I'd improve

As a next step, I would add a dedicated usability testing phase:

  • Learner interviews before and after using the tool
  • Observing users as they navigate, measuring task completion and points of confusion
  • Testing whether learners understand feedback and instructions
  • Comparing experiences across levels of digital literacy
  • Accessibility checks for visual, auditory, and motor needs
  • Co-designing future versions with learners and teachers
  • A mobile or web version that still works on low bandwidth
10

Reflection

This project taught me that educational technology design is both a learning challenge and an equity challenge. A tool can be instructionally effective and still exclude learners if it assumes strong infrastructure, high digital literacy, or English-only navigation.

Inclusive tools must be judged not only by learning outcomes, but by how accessible, understandable, and empowering they are for the learners who need them most.
